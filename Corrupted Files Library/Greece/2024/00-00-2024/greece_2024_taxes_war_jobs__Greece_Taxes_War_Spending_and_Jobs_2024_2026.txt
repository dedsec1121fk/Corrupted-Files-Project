Article

Greece Taxes, War Spending, and Jobs: 2024-2026

Why this file exists:
In Greece, distrust is often not abstract.
People ask whether taxes, austerity habits, defence burdens, EU/NATO obligations, and weak job quality are all part of the same pressure system.

This file is not a claim that every euro is stolen.
It is a record of tax burden, defence prioritization, labor stress, and the policy response.

1) Greece remains a relatively heavy-tax labour economy.
The OECD says the tax wedge for the average single worker in Greece was 39.3% in 2024,
up from 38.7% in 2023 and above the OECD average of 34.9%.
That means the labour tax burden on a standard worker remains high by OECD standards.
Key link:
- https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/04/taxing-wages-2025-country-notes_16d47563/greece_5039ed6c/ffc83bef-en.pdf

2) Greece still carries a large defence burden by alliance standards.
NATO's 2024 estimates show Greece at 3.08% of GDP on defence,
well above the old 2% NATO guideline.
The same NATO tables show Greek defence expenditure rising from 4,358 million euros in 2014 to 6,792 million euros in 2024.
Key link:
- https://www.nato.int/content/dam/nato/legacy-wcm/media_pdf/2024/6/pdf/240617-def-exp-2024-en.pdf

3) NATO's newer path points to more security spending pressure, not less.
NATO says Allies committed in 2025 to move toward 5% of GDP annually by 2035,
with at least 3.5% for core defence and up to 1.5% for security-related spending.
For a country like Greece, already structurally above 2%, that matters.
Key link:
- https://www.nato.int/en/what-we-do/introduction-to-nato/defence-expenditures-and-natos-5-commitment

4) The Greek state itself expects defence pressure to rise.
Greece's 2026 Draft Budgetary Plan says defence expenditure as a share of GDP is estimated to increase by 0.1 percentage points from 2024 to 2025
and by 0.3 percentage points from 2024 to 2026.
Key link:
- https://minfin.gov.gr/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Greece_DBP_2026_final.pdf

5) The labor market is improving on paper but remains strained.
The OECD said labour shortages remained high in Greece even as the unemployment rate fell to 8.2% in September 2025.
It also said wage-cost growth accelerated to 8.7% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2025.
This suggests a stressed labor market, not a settled one.
Key link:
- https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-economic-outlook-volume-2025-issue-2_9f653ca1-en/full-report/greece_ec79a5a5.html

6) The policy answer is more monitoring, reskilling, and performance control.
The OECD's 2025 review of Greece's “Jobs Again” reform says the reform uses performance-based funding,
digital infrastructure, provider oversight, individual learning accounts, and closer alignment of training with labour-market needs.
That is not automatically bad.
But it does show a technocratic response to labor insecurity: track, certify, score, retrain.
Key link:
- https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/10/the-jobs-again-reform-in-greece_ed9bfcc7/bff1cb60-en.pdf

7) Greece sits inside a wider AI and skills shift.
The IMF's 2026 work on new skills says advanced economies are already seeing strong demand for AI- and IT-related skills,
and roughly 1 in 10 job postings in advanced economies require at least one new skill.
Southern and eastern European economies sit in the middle zone of future skills pressure and supply constraints.
Key link:
- https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/sdn/2026/english/sdnea2026001.pdf

The strongest “connect-the-dots” frame for Greece is not just corruption.
It is a stacked pressure model:
high labor-tax burden, persistent defence commitments, a labor market that is still fragile, and a state response built around digitalized retraining and compliance.
That can feel dystopian even when each policy is presented as neutral modernization.

Extra context:
This file is not claiming that taxes automatically equal malice. The point is structural: when citizens face a high labor tax wedge, fragile wages, housing pressure, and labor-market insecurity while military or security expenditure remains politically protected, distrust grows. In Greece, OECD tax-wedge data, NATO defense-spending figures, and the debt story together feed the feeling that ordinary people pay first while power centers remain insulated.
