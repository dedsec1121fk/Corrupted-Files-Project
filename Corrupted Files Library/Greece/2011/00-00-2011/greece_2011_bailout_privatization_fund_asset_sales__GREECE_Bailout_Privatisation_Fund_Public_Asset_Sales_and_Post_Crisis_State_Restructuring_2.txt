Summary:
This file tracks the post-debt-crisis system that tied Greece's recovery to privatisations, public-asset restructuring, and the long management chain around HRADF, HFSF, and later HCAP / sovereign-wealth-fund logic. The strongest claim here is not that every asset sale was corrupt. It is that bailout-era governance shifted major decisions over public assets into a high-pressure framework built around debt reduction, creditor demands, and institutional restructuring from above.

What is documented:
- EU compliance reporting says Greece adopted legislation to establish HCAP, a privatisation and investment fund that would hold subsidiaries including HRADF and HFSF.
- Reuters reported in 2023 that Greece had raised about 10 billion euros from state asset sales since 2011.
- Reuters reported in 2024 that Greece planned its first sovereign wealth fund to sell state assets such as properties, ports, and public utilities not sold during the debt crisis.
- Reuters reported in late 2024 that the country completed the re-privatisation of major banks by selling down bailout-era holdings.

Why it matters:
The public criticism is not only about corruption. It is also about democratic distance. In crisis conditions, public assets can be reorganized and sold under intense fiscal pressure, while ordinary citizens mainly experience the consequences rather than the decision-making power.

What this archive does NOT claim:
- It does NOT claim every sale was illegitimate.
- It does NOT claim privatisation itself is automatically a scandal.
- It does claim that the scale and institutional design of the post-bailout asset regime mattered politically and socially.

Timeline:
- 2011: HRADF era begins amid debt-crisis emergency logic.
- 2015-2016: Third bailout framework deepens privatisation commitments.
- 2016: HCAP legislation adopted under bailout review.
- 2023: Greece targets record state-asset sales.
- 2024: Government announces sovereign-wealth-fund plan for unsold assets.
- 2024-2026: Bank re-privatisations and remaining state-asset strategy continue.
