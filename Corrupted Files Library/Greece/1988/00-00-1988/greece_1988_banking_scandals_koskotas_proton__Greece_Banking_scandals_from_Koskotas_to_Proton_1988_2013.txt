Summary:
Greece's late-1980s Koskotas scandal and the later Proton Bank scandal are different cases from different periods, but together they show a recurring pattern: private financial abuse, political fallout, media power, and public suspicion that elite networks protect themselves until the damage is too large to hide.

Koskotas / Bank of Crete:
- Reuters' historical background notes that Andreas Papandreou's era was tarnished by the Koskotas case involving embezzlement at the Bank of Crete.
- Contemporary TIME reporting described accusations that large sums missing from the bank had been used for payoffs to PASOK-linked figures, though many of the strongest allegations were heavily disputed.
- The case exploded into one of the most damaging political-financial crises of late-1980s Greece.

Proton Bank:
- Reuters reported in 2012 that Greek prosecutors alleged businessman Lavrentis Lavrentiadis turned Proton into a "bank of cronies."
- Reuters also reported accusations that Proton issued more than 600 million euros in bad loans to connected companies.
- Lavrentiadis denied wrongdoing, which is important to preserve in any serious archive.

Why it matters:
- These cases feed the Greek belief that banking, party power, regulators, and media influence can become entangled.
- They show why distrust of institutional clean-up mechanisms persisted into the debt-crisis era.

What is proved / what is not:
- PROVED: both cases became major public scandals with judicial and political consequences.
- PROVED: Proton involved serious prosecutorial allegations about connected lending and misuse.
- NOT PROVED: every sensational allegation surrounding Koskotas. Some of the biggest claims remained contested.
