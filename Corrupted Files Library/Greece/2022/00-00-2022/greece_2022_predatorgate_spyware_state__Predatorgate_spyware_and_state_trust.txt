ARTICLE

Predatorgate is one of Greece’s central digital-era rule-of-law scandals. It involves spyware, journalists, politicians, civil society, intelligence-service wiretapping, political accountability, and the question of whether modern states can secretly turn phones into evidence machines against their own democratic environment.

The scandal erupted in 2022 when targets discovered attempts or infections involving Predator spyware. The European Parliament later described the scandal as involving journalists, politicians, and civil society representatives, and noted that in many cases Predator targeting appeared in parallel with legitimate wiretapping by the state’s intelligence services. That parallel is what made the case explosive. If a person is both legally wiretapped and targeted by illegal spyware, the public naturally asks whether two systems were operating near each other: one official, one deniable.

Spyware is different from ordinary phone tapping. A modern phone contains messages, photos, microphones, cameras, location histories, contacts, passwords, and the private life of everyone who communicates with the owner. Compromising a phone can expose sources, family, strategy, health, finance, and political plans. Against a journalist, it can reveal whistleblowers. Against an opposition politician, it can distort democracy. Against a businessperson, it can become economic intelligence.

Digital surveillance hides behind technical complexity. Citizens cannot easily inspect their phones. Courts may lack access to classified material. Companies operate across borders. Spyware vendors sell through intermediaries. Governments deny direct use or blame private actors. By the time a public investigation begins, devices may be replaced, logs lost, and responsibility blurred.

In 2026, the European Parliament’s agenda page said four individuals had been sentenced in Greece for violating the confidentiality of telephone communications and illegally accessing personal data and conversations, with sentences suspended pending appeal. It also said the broader debate concerned rule-of-law standards, judicial independence, fundamental rights, and safeguards against surveillance abuse. The same page recorded that the Greek Supreme Court cleared Greek intelligence services and political officials of wrongdoing in July 2024. Those facts show the split public reality: legal processes produced some convictions while major political questions remained disputed.

Conclusion: Predatorgate is not only a spyware story. It is a state-capacity story. Democracies now need surveillance transparency strong enough for the smartphone age: notification of targets after investigations close, independent technical labs, strict procurement records, penalties for illegal spyware possession or use, parliamentary oversight with teeth, and protection for journalists and opposition figures. Without that, surveillance becomes a ghost institution — visible through fear, invisible through paperwork.
