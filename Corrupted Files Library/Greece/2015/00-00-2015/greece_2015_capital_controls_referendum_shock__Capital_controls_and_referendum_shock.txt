1. The story
In 2015, Greece held a referendum in which voters rejected bailout terms, but the country soon
accepted a new bailout framework under extreme financial pressure. Banks were closed, capital
controls restricted daily life, and the meaning of democratic choice became painfully unclear.
The event belongs in the database because it shows how sovereignty can be squeezed by debt,
banking dependence, and emergency liquidity.

2. Background
The Greek debt crisis was already years old. Austerity had damaged households, unemployment was
severe, and trust in old parties had collapsed. Syriza rose by promising resistance to the
bailout order. But Greece remained inside the euro system, dependent on banks, creditors, and
European institutions. The referendum became a direct collision between popular will and
financial structure.

3. Chronology and turning points
- January 2015: Syriza came to power promising a different path in bailout negotiations.
- June–July 2015: negotiations broke down; banks closed and capital controls were imposed.
- 5 July 2015: voters rejected the proposed bailout terms in a referendum.
- After the referendum, Greece accepted a new bailout agreement under pressure of financial
collapse and euro exit risk.
- Capital controls and economic consequences continued, leaving a long memory of humiliation and
constraint.

4. Psychological / social-control angle
The psychological effect was democratic injury. Citizens were asked a question, answered loudly,
and then watched reality move in the opposite direction. Some saw betrayal; others saw
unavoidable survival. Either way, the event taught a brutal lesson: in a debt crisis, ballot
boxes operate inside systems of money that voters do not directly control.

5. What is documented
- The referendum occurred and the “No” vote won.
- Banks closed and capital controls affected ordinary citizens.
- A new bailout agreement followed despite the referendum result.

6. What remains disputed, exaggerated, or unresolved
- Supporters and critics disagree over whether the government betrayed the vote or avoided
national catastrophe.
- European institutions and Greek leaders assign responsibility differently.

7. Why this belongs in Corrupted Files
The 2015 file belongs here because it is not only economics. It is about who governs when banks
close, who has leverage when money stops, and how democratic legitimacy survives when the public
feels trapped between poverty and external command.
