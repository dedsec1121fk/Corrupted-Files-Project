Why this file exists:
A lot of surveillance power in the United States has not depended on dramatic courtroom scenes. Some of it has operated through administrative instruments, secrecy rules, and gag orders that kept targets and the public in the dark.

What is documented:
- DOJ's Office of Inspector General found repeated FBI problems in National Security Letter practice, including improper requests and cases where recipients provided more information than was legally requested.
- The OIG review said one matter involved receipt of information when there was no open national-security investigation, and another involved credit-report material not permitted by law.
- DOJ's 2023 FISA memorandum states that the President directed the Attorney General to amend rules for the gag orders accompanying NSLs so that the secrecy would not be indefinite.
- That is important because it confirms the basic structure: secret record demands plus built-in restrictions on what recipients could say.

Why it matters:
- NSLs represent the "bureaucratic" face of the surveillance state: paperwork, secrecy, and low public visibility rather than movie-style raids.
- The danger is not just collection. It is the asymmetry: the government knows it asked, the company knows it received the demand, and the public often learns little or much later.

What is NOT proved:
- This file does not show that every NSL was illegal.
- It does show that the legal tool was powerful, secrecy-heavy, and mishandled often enough to justify long-term distrust.

Logical reading:
- The issue is not whether one likes or dislikes intelligence work. The issue is whether a democracy can tolerate a large class of hidden demands for data that historically came with broad secrecy and recurring compliance problems.
