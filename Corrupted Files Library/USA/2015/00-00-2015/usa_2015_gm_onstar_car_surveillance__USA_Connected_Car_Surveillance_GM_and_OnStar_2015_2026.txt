USA: Connected-Car Surveillance, GM, and OnStar (2015-2026)

1) Cars as data-collection devices and location/behavior monetization
Status: [DOCUMENTED]

Summary:
The FTC's 2025-2026 action against General Motors and OnStar turned a once-dismissed fear into a documented modern surveillance case: the ordinary consumer vehicle as a rolling sensor package that can collect, infer, package, and sell sensitive movement and behavior data.

Why it matters:
This is a dystopian shift from surveillance through phones and apps to surveillance through infrastructure and products people rely on in daily life. Cars can reveal work patterns, worship habits, clinic visits, protests, social contacts, and habits of movement.

What is proved:
- The FTC alleged that GM and OnStar collected, used, and sold drivers' precise geolocation and driving-behavior data without adequately obtaining consumers' informed consent.
- The FTC's 2026 final order imposed a five-year ban on GM sharing precise geolocation and driver-behavior data with consumer-reporting agencies.
- The complaint and order materials show how connected-vehicle systems can become monetization pipelines far beyond navigation or safety assistance.
- FTC materials define location data broadly enough to highlight how "deidentified" claims can fail when movement data can still be tied back to a vehicle or person.

What this archive does NOT claim:
- It does NOT claim every carmaker engaged in the exact same practices.
- It does NOT claim the FTC order proves all connected-car services are illegitimate. The documented point is that the surveillance/business model itself is real.

Detailed timeline:
- Mid-2010s: GM equips newer vehicles with increasingly data-rich connected systems.
- January 2025: FTC announces proposed action over alleged collection and sale of precise geolocation and driving-behavior data without proper consent.
- January 2026: FTC finalizes the order, highlighting an "egregious betrayal" of consumer trust and imposing restrictions and compliance obligations.
