1. Why this file exists
The Business Plot file belongs here because it sits between documented congressional attention and enduring uncertainty about how real the alleged plan was.

2. Background and setting
During the Great Depression, social anger, veteran mobilization and fear of radical politics created an unstable environment. Retired Marine Corps Major General Smedley Butler claimed that wealthy interests had approached him as a potential figurehead for a veterans’ movement that could pressure or displace the Roosevelt administration. The congressional record did not prove a full coup machine, but it did treat parts of Butler’s testimony as credible enough to investigate.

3. Chronology
- 1933–1934: Butler reports approaches and claims a plot existed.
- 1934: The McCormack-Dickstein Committee hears testimony.
- Afterward: the story becomes a recurring warning about private power and authoritarian temptation in crisis.

4. What happened in practical terms
The alleged mechanism was not tanks in the streets but elite financing, veteran symbolism, media management and political pressure. Its power as a case study is exactly that: democratic breakdown can be imagined as respectable influence first, not open violence first.

5. Evidence map
Documented elements:
- Butler testified publicly.
- A congressional committee examined the claims.
- No completed coup was proven and prosecutions did not follow.

Alleged or disputed elements:
- How organized the alleged backers were.
- Whether some named interests were serious actors or only talkative intermediaries.

Unsupported or overextended claims to treat carefully:
- Claims that the United States came within hours of a fascist takeover are stronger than the documentary record supports.

6. Human impact
The human impact is indirect but important: a society in depression can become vulnerable to men who promise order, status and rescue from humiliation.

7. Why the case created suspicion
Secrecy, elite networks and lack of prosecution kept the story alive. People suspect what they cannot audit.

8. Counterpoints and caution
A careful reader should neither dismiss it as nonsense nor inflate it into proven overthrow. It is best read as a credible warning signal from a fragile decade.

9. What a reader should conclude
The durable conclusion is that crises make democratic institutions vulnerable to private power, paramilitary imagery and “emergency” rhetoric.
