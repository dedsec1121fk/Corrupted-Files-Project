Why this file matters:
Not every dystopian process is a secret intelligence operation. Sometimes the "conspiracy" is slower and more ordinary: industry profits, weak regulation, partial science denial, and decades of delay before the public is protected.

Core record:
- NIOSH says that in the 1920s the production of tetraethyl lead (TEL) gasoline led to 15 worker fatalities and many psychotic episodes.
- NIOSH describes the leaded-gasoline era as a four-decade tug-of-war between science, industry interests, and politics.
- The U.S. Energy Information Administration says health hazards associated with lead were documented from the early 1920s; in 1927 the Surgeon General set only a voluntary standard for lead content.
- EPA history records the long phaseout path: early controls in the 1970s, tighter standards in the 1980s, and the final step in the phaseout of leaded gasoline in 1996.
- CDC-linked historical reviews show blood lead levels fell sharply after federal regulation and source control.

Why this belongs in the archive:
The important point is not hidden aliens or one secret cabal. It is that the public-health harm was real, known in important respects, and still allowed to continue for decades because money, politics, and institutional inertia beat precaution.

What is proved:
- Worker poisonings were real.
- Long-term atmospheric lead exposure from gasoline was real.
- Federal regulation eventually drove major reductions in exposure.

What remains debated less than before:
- The exact degree of what different actors knew at each early stage can vary by source.
- The big picture is not really disputed anymore: serious harm existed long before full protection arrived.

Archive logic:
This is a "slow fall" file. It shows how a society can normalize mass low-level poisoning while arguments, lobbying, and half-measures drag on for decades.
