USA: Teapot Dome and the Oil-Lease Corruption Scandal (1921-1929)

1) Secret leases, bribes, and the classic template for modern corruption scandals
Status: [DOCUMENTED]

Summary:
Teapot Dome is one of the clearest early U.S. examples of what people now describe as a “deep-state style” corruption scandal, though in plain terms it was a cabinet-level corruption and cover-up case. Interior Secretary Albert B. Fall secretly leased U.S. naval petroleum reserves at Teapot Dome, Wyoming, and Elk Hills, California, to private oil companies without competitive bidding. Senate investigators later exposed the arrangement and uncovered bribery and “sweetheart” dealing tied to the leases.

Why it matters:
Teapot Dome matters because it shows that high-level corruption, hidden arrangements with private industry, and state capture are not new. It also helped establish the Senate’s oversight power as a real instrument against executive secrecy.

Timeline:
- 1921-1922: Naval petroleum reserves are transferred to the Interior Department and secretly leased without competitive bidding.
- 1922: Senator John Kendrick introduces the resolution that launches the Senate inquiry.
- 1920s: Investigators uncover evidence of loans, gifts, and corrupt financial benefits flowing to Interior Secretary Albert Fall.
- Fall becomes the first former U.S. cabinet member sent to prison for crimes committed in office.

What is proved:
- The leases were secretly arranged without competitive bidding.
- Senate investigators exposed the arrangement and documented corruption at the highest levels.
- Teapot Dome became a lasting symbol of government corruption linked to private money and resource extraction.

What this file does NOT claim:
- It does not claim one scandal proves every later conspiracy theory.
- It uses Teapot Dome as a documented historical anchor for how corruption, secrecy, and elite access interact.
