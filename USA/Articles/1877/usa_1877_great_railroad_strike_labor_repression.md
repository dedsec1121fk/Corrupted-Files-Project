## English — Summary

The Great Railroad Strike of 1877 spread across multiple states after wage cuts and became the first major national labor uprising in the United States, met by militias, police and federal troops.

## English — Full Story

- Background — The depression that followed the Panic of 1873 weakened workers, while railroad companies imposed repeated wage cuts and maintained dangerous, disciplined workplaces.

- Central episode — A walkout began in Martinsburg, West Virginia, in July 1877 and spread along rail networks to Baltimore, Pittsburgh, Chicago, St. Louis and other cities, sometimes accompanied by mass protest, clashes and property destruction.

- Immediate response — State governments deployed militias, some of which refused orders or confronted crowds violently. President Rutherford B. Hayes sent federal troops to protect rail movement and restore control.

- Human and institutional impact — Dozens were killed, many were injured, rail property was destroyed and the strike exposed the national scale of labor grievances and the state's willingness to defend transportation and corporate order.

- Accountability — The case requires city-by-city analysis because casualties, militia behavior, crowd composition and property destruction varied, while federal intervention raised lasting civil-military questions.

- Legacy — The strike accelerated labor organization and encouraged elites to build armories and security systems, making it a foundational conflict in U.S. labor and policing history.
