## English — Summary

In November 1898 white supremacists in Wilmington, North Carolina, used propaganda, armed violence and forced resignations to overthrow a legitimately elected multiracial local government.

## English — Full Story

- Background — Black political participation and a Fusion coalition of Republicans and Populists challenged Democratic control. Newspapers and political organizers promoted a white-supremacy campaign before the election.

- Central episode — On 10 November armed white men burned the Black-owned Daily Record newspaper, killed Black residents, drove people from the city and forced elected officials to resign.

- Immediate response — Coup leaders installed their own government and faced little immediate legal accountability. State-level disfranchisement measures then restricted Black voting for generations.

- Human and institutional impact — People were killed, families fled, property and political power were seized and a thriving Black community suffered long-term demographic and economic loss.

- Accountability — The event demands analysis of political leaders, newspapers, armed clubs, business interests, state authorities and the institutional failure to prosecute an open overthrow.

- Legacy — Wilmington is widely recognized as a coup, not merely a riot, and is central to understanding how violence built Jim Crow political power.
