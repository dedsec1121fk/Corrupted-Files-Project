## English — Summary

In 1972 Native activists crossed the United States in the Trail of Broken Treaties and occupied the Bureau of Indian Affairs in Washington after negotiations and accommodation plans failed.

## English — Full Story

- Background — American Indian organizations challenged termination policy, poverty, police violence, treaty violations and federal control. The caravan carried a Twenty-Point Position Paper demanding restoration of treaty-based sovereignty.

- Central episode — Caravans reached Washington shortly before the 1972 election. After logistical disputes and failed access to officials, participants entered and occupied the BIA headquarters for nearly a week.

- Immediate response — Federal officials negotiated an end to the occupation, provided travel assistance and agreed to review demands, while records were removed or damaged amid the chaotic occupation.

- Human and institutional impact — The action brought national attention to treaty rights and federal administration, but also produced controversy over property damage, documents and relations among Native organizations.

- Accountability — The case requires attention to the government's failed preparation, protesters' internal diversity, handling of records, negotiation promises and the long history of federal treaty noncompliance.

- Legacy — The Trail helped move treaty sovereignty into national debate and formed part of the broader Red Power era that reshaped Indigenous activism and federal policy.
