1. The story
In 1971, activists broke into an FBI office in Media, Pennsylvania, stole files, and mailed them
to journalists. The documents helped reveal COINTELPRO, the FBI’s secret programs against
political groups. The act was illegal, but the records exposed something the public had a right
to know: a domestic security apparatus was not merely investigating crimes; it was disrupting
political life.

2. Background
By the late 1960s, the FBI had enormous power and secrecy. Civil-rights groups, antiwar
activists, Black liberation organizations, student groups, and others were under surveillance or
disruption. Before the burglary, many citizens suspected abuses but lacked proof. The leaked
files turned suspicion into documentation and forced journalists to decide whether the public
interest outweighed official secrecy.

3. Chronology and turning points
- 1960s: FBI domestic intelligence operations targeted a wide range of political movements.
- March 1971: the Citizens’ Commission to Investigate the FBI burglarized the Media office while
many Americans were distracted by a major boxing match.
- Files were sent to journalists and lawmakers.
- The word COINTELPRO entered public debate through document exposure.
- The revelations helped build momentum for the Church Committee investigations in the mid-1970s.

4. Psychological / social-control angle
The case shows how secrecy changes the psychology of citizenship. If people suspect they are
watched, they may self-censor even without direct punishment. If agencies can disrupt lawful
organizing, fear becomes a political tool. The burglary also raises a hard ethical question:
when institutions hide wrongdoing, do leaks become the only path to accountability?

5. What is documented
- The Media burglary happened and the FBI searched for the burglars for years without catching
them at the time.
- Leaked files contributed to public understanding of COINTELPRO.
- COINTELPRO operations included disruption, infiltration, and attempts to create conflict within
groups.

6. What remains disputed, exaggerated, or unresolved
- The burglary itself was illegal and should not be romanticized without moral caution.
- Some targeted groups included individuals involved in crime or violence, but COINTELPRO reached
far beyond individualized criminal investigation.

7. Why this belongs in Corrupted Files
This case belongs here because it shows the paradox of democratic secrecy: the official system
may classify evidence of its own misconduct, leaving citizens dependent on whistleblowers,
leaks, or illegal exposure. A free society should not need burglars to discover what its
security agencies do in its name.
